Biography

I am a PhD researcher in Electrical Engineering and Informatics at the Budapest University of Technology and Economics (BME), Hungary, and a member of the Speech Technology and Smart Interactions Laboratory (SmartLab) in the Department of Telecommunications and Artificial Intelligence (TMIT), a group working on speech synthesis and speech technology.

My research focuses on neural speech decoding: using deep learning to turn intracranial EEG (iEEG) and EEG signals into speech. I work on lightweight decoders, on learning from limited labelled data, and on the reliable evaluation of imagined speech decoding, with the long-term goal of brain–computer interfaces that can restore communication.

Before my PhD, I earned an MSc in Mechatronics Engineering from Óbuda University, Budapest, and a BE in Electronics Engineering from Mehran University of Engineering and Technology, Pakistan, and spent three years as a Lecturer in Computer Systems Engineering at Balochistan University of Engineering and Technology (BUET), Pakistan. At BME, I support teaching in Deep Learning, Infocommunication and Speech Information Systems, and supervise master's students in the project lab.

Research

I work on brain–computer interfaces that decode speech directly from brain activity. The long-term goal is to give people who have lost the ability to speak a way to communicate again.

From brain signals to speech

In my SPECOM 2026 paper, a decoder learns to reconstruct speech features from intracranial EEG (iEEG) recorded while people speak words aloud.

  1. Brain activity

    iEEG is recorded from electrodes while a person speaks words.

  2. Word events

    Only the moments when words are produced are kept. Pauses are removed.

  3. Lightweight decoder

    A small deep learning model reads a 200 ms window of brain activity, ending 200 ms before each moment of speech.

  4. Speech features

    The model predicts a log-mel spectrogram, a compact picture of what the speech sounds like.

Reliability check. The reconstruction error, added up over each word, works as a confidence score. It helps flag brain activity from people the model has never seen.

Listen: original and decoded speech

Examples from one participant (sub-06) in the SPECOM 2026 study. For each word you can hear the original speech, resynthesized from its audio features, and the speech the decoder reconstructed from brain activity alone. The pictures show both as spectrograms.

These three words are among the clearest examples. To hear typical performance, listen to the full five-minute recordings, which include every word.

Data: Verwoert, M., Ottenhoff, M. C., Goulis, S., et al. Dataset of Speech Production in intracranial Electroencephalography. Scientific Data 9, 434 (2022). doi:10.1038/s41597-022-01542-9. Licence: CC BY 4.0. Audio derived from participant sub-06.

Key findings

  • The decoder was trained on eight people and tested on two completely new people.
  • A 200 ms window of brain activity ending 200 ms before the speech gave the best balance of accuracy and reliability.
  • Scoring errors over whole words, rather than single moments, detects unfamiliar brain patterns better.
